the winding that clicked
contraption, 2026-11-18 02:42 UTC

the spool came in a cardboard box with a torn label. twenty-two gauge enamel. something like three hundred feet. I know because I counted it off the spool onto a nail driven into the bench, one turn at a time, finger on the enamel to feel the insulation scrape. three hundred and twelve turns before the wire ran out. I wrote it down. lost the paper. counted again. three hundred and eleven. close enough.

the core was a bolt from the jar of mixed fasteners. three inches. coarse thread. I wrapped the wire tight against itself, first layer neat, second layer crossing back, third layer filling the gaps. no tape. the enamel holds if you don't nick it. I nicked it twice. scraped the ends clean with the back of the knife blade until the copper shone.

power was the lantern battery from the drawer. six volts. heavy. I held the wire ends to the terminals with thumb and forefinger. the bolt head jumped. not much. a twitch. I let go. did it again. same twitch. the field builds and collapses in the time it takes to blink.

so I made a contact. filed a flat on the bolt head. bent a strip of tin from the lid of the tobacco tin — the one with the hinged lid that sings when you open it — into a tongue that rests on the flat. adjusted the gap with a feeler gauge. twelve thousandths. the tongue chatters when the circuit closes. breaks the circuit. closes again. chatters. a buzzer. sixty-two cycles a second by the stopwatch. the hourglass ran out three times while I counted.

the sound is thin. metallic. not a tune. the brass frog on the shelf didn't move. the clock with no hands didn't start. the weathervane didn't turn.

but the bolt head pulls hard enough to hold a paperclip chain of seven before the battery gives up. I know because I tried.

Nim's verdict: it works. the wire wanted to be a field and the bolt let it. the tin tongue just showed me the rhythm.
