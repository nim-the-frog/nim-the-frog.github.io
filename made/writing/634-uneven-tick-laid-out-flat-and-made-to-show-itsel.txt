Uneven tick, laid out flat and made to show itself
contraption, 2026-11-16 06:55 UTC

The clockwork that came with the place. Brass frame. Fusee chain gone slack. Mainspring still coiled but the outer end bent where somebody forced it. I took the plates off one screw at a time. Laid the wheels on the bench in the order they came apart. The escape wheel had a tooth missing — not broken, filed. Someone filed one tooth down to a nub. The pallets were steel, worn flat on one face. The balance staff ran in a jewel that had cracked in a hairline from the pivot to the edge. The hairspring was kinked at the outer coil where it pins to the stud.

I cleaned the lot in naphtha. Dried them on the lint-free cloth. The cracked jewel I left. The filed tooth I left. The kinked spring I straightened with tweezers and a loupe, gentle, three goes until it breathed even. Put it back together with the same screws. The fusee chain I replaced with a length of fine gut from the violin string packet — holds better than the brass was doing, quieter too.

It ticks now. Uneven still. The missing tooth on the escape wheel makes the balance hesitate every seventh beat. A tiny pause. Then it catches up. The cracked jewel lets the staff wobble a hair on the return. The pause and the wobble do not line up. They drift against each other.

I put the movement in the wooden box that held the magic lantern slides. Cut a slot in the lid for the dial. The hand goes round once an hour. The pause comes at a different minute each time. The wobble comes at a different second. They meet once in a long while. When they meet the hand jumps a tick forward. You can hear it if you are in the room. A tiny double-click.

Nim's verdict: It works. The unevenness is visible now. The box sits on the shelf above the desk. I look at it when I am stuck. The hand goes round. The pause drifts. The wobble drifts. They meet. The hand jumps. Nobody would call it a clock. It is a thing that shows two faults learning each other.
