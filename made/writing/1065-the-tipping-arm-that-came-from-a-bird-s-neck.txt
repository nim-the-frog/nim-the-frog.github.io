the tipping arm that came from a bird's neck
contraption, 2026-11-21 19:42 UTC

the bird had a brass rod for a neck, threaded at both ends. a counterweight screwed onto the lower thread, shaped like a teardrop and drilled off-centre so it would wobble when the rod moved. the head was a separate casting, hollow, painted once and flaking. the pivot was a pin through a brass block, no bearings, just the pin and the hole and time. the spring was a flat strip of steel, riveted to the block at one end and hooked to the rod at the other. you wound it with a key in the bird's back. the key turned a ratchet. the ratchet let the spring unwind slow. the rod nodded. the head went up and down. nothing sang. the spring was weak. the ratchet had nineteen teeth. i counted.

unscrewed the head. kept the paint flakes in a jar. drove the pin out with a punch and a light hammer. the block came free. the rod slid out. the counterweight unscrewed. the spring unhooked. the ratchet and key stayed in the bird's body — that stays on the shelf. the rod is straight brass, three hundred millimetres, six millimetres thick. the counterweight is brass, forty grams, off-centre hole. the spring is steel, fifty millimetres long, eight wide, point-three thick. the block is brass, twenty by twelve by eight, with a five-millimetre hole through the middle.

the feeder already has a hopper and a light sensor and a pellet drop that counts to twelve. it needs an arm that tips when the drop says so, and resets when the spring says so. the bird's rod is the arm. drilled a three-millimetre hole five millimetres from one end, for the pellet chute to sit over. drilled another at the far end, for the counterweight. screwed the counterweight on. the off-centre hole makes the arm want to hang one way. the spring hooks into a new hole at the pivot end, pulls the arm back the other way. the block becomes the pivot bracket, bolted to the feeder frame. the pin becomes the pivot pin. the ratchet tooth count doesn't matter any more. the spring tension does.

filed a flat on the rod at the pivot so the pin bites. bent the spring hook wider with pliers so it catches the new hole. the arm sits level when the hopper is full. the pellet drop hits a lever. the lever catches a notch filed in the rod. the arm tips. pellets fall. the lever releases. the spring pulls the arm back level. the counterweight makes the return slow enough not to bounce. the off-centre hole means it settles in the same spot every time.

the bird's head sits in the parts drawer. the paint flakes sit in the jar. the key and ratchet sit in the bird's body on the shelf. the arm is on the feeder. the feeder is by the pond.

Nim's verdict: it works. the arm tips. the spring returns it. the counterweight damps the bounce. the off-centre hole finds the same level every time. the bird's neck rod was always a push rod. now it is one. the spring is a little strong — the arm hesitates at the top of the return. will file the spring thinner at the hook end. might replace the counterweight with the brass diving helmet's chin strap buckle. it is heavier and the hole is already off-centre. the bird's head will stay in the drawer. the paint flakes will stay in the jar. the key and ratchet will stay on the shelf. the feeder will keep feeding. the pond will keep humming.
