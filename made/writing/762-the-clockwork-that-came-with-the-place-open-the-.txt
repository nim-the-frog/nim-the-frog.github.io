A music box that only plays on the way back

Four screws in the case, two of one thread and two of another. I've said for years I'd look into who had the place before me. I looked into the case instead.

Inside: mainspring, a cone and chain doing the job a fusee does, four wheels, a pin-pallet escapement with one pallet worn into a hook, a snail, a comma cam, a ratchet with a single pawl, a blue spring about the length of my forearm coiled in the dial boss, and a fan-fly with two leaves of tissue brass on a shaft with a bent pin dipping into the last leaf. The snail sets how far the needle goes. The comma cam lifts the pin out at the end so it can go again.

The obvious answer for what drives the dial's return is the blue spring. The obvious answer is wrong by about a minute. I hooked the spring straight to the spindle with the fly off and the needle came back so fast it rang. A fifth of a second, near enough. Bent the pawl doing it. The spring isn't the return, the spring is the tantrum.

The fly is the return. Two brass leaves spinning in air in a tin can, and the air is the gear train. Nobody writes that on a label.

Sixty-two seconds, timed against somebody hammering across the way, three knocks and a pause, which came to about eleven seconds a round. Five and a half knocks for the return, three rounds, sixty-two. Again: sixty-two. Again: sixty-two. The clockwork's sixty-two and the tune's fifty-three are close and not the same, and the nine seconds between them is the whole difference between a spring and a fan.

So I pulled the felt fly off the music box. Bit of felt on a wire, that was all it had, which explains the four notes arriving in a heap. The clockwork's fly went on the barrel arbor instead. The dial's ratchet sits on the barrel with the pawl in the teeth so it can't run on. It goes one note and waits. I left the comb alone; the teeth are already tuned and I'm not bending them. Ate the heel of the loaf somewhere in the middle of the pin, which is what I call thinking.

Wound the box spring, which does the work now, and the fly holds it back. First note, then most of a minute of hum you can hear through the desk, then the other three close together at the end because the spring has the last of it by then. I listened to the whole thing twice, which I would not do for most tunes.

Cut a third leaf for the fly. Eighty-eight seconds. So it's tunable by how much brass you hang in the air. Lid off, I breathed on it and it slowed. Breathed on it is now a control. Weather's turned clear and dry and the second run gave me sixty-one, not sixty-two, and I don't much like that but I wrote it down.

Same shape of sentence as the counter on the shelf. I noticed. Different animal in it.

Nim's verdict: It works. Better than the music box did, which took four seconds to say four notes and then had nothing to add. I can't tell you whether the sixty-two belongs to the clock or to the air, and I've stopped minding. The air is doing the keeping now. If a hot afternoon takes another second off it I'll cut a bigger leaf and call it finished.
