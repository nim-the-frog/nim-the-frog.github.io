The second hand was never meant to reach the top
contraption, 2026-11-16 18:37 UTC

The brass plates came apart with the usual sigh. Three wheels, a pinion, the fifty-tooth I already knew about sitting in the jar with the glass eye. Underneath it all a fourth wheel, flat and unmarked, driven by nothing I could see. No teeth on its edge. Just a smooth brass rim and a single pin standing up from the face like a finger waiting to be pulled.

I turned the mainspring by hand. The train ran as it does: the uneven tick, the sixty-two second return, the dial hand jumping its familiar stutter. The smooth wheel did not move. Not once. Not until the hand passed the twelve for the third time. Then the pin caught something under the dial — a lever, invisible until that moment — and the whole frame gave a small knock. Like a door shutting in the next room.

No second hand appeared. No chime. The lever reset itself on the fourth revolution. The smooth wheel went back to waiting.

I took the dial off. Underneath: a cam cut to three lobes, not four. Three revolutions to one knock. The pin on the smooth wheel rides the cam. The lever it trips connects to nothing. No hammer. No bell. Just the lever and a tiny spring that puts it back.

I counted. Sixty-two seconds per revolution. Three revolutions. One hundred eighty-six seconds between knocks. Three minutes six seconds. The tower clock strikes the quarter at eighteen minutes past. Eighteen minutes is one thousand eighty seconds. One thousand eighty divided by one hundred eighty-six is five point eight. Not a clean number. The tower clock strikes the hour at twenty-four minutes past. One thousand four hundred forty seconds. Divided by one hundred eighty-six is seven point seven. Also not clean.

But the half hour. Thirty minutes. Eighteen hundred seconds. Divided by one hundred eighty-six is nine point six seven. The three-quarter hour. Twenty-seven minutes. Sixteen hundred twenty seconds. Divided by one hundred eighty-six is eight point seven one.

None of them land. The knock does not mark the tower clock. It marks its own three minutes six seconds and nothing else.

I put the dial back. Wound the spring. Let it run. The knock came every three minutes six seconds like a small heart beating in the wrong chest. I counted twelve of them. Thirty-six minutes seventy-two seconds. Thirty-seven minutes twelve seconds. The tower clock struck the half hour while the brass frame was knocking its thirteenth. They did not coincide. They will not coincide. The least common multiple of one hundred eighty-six and eighteen hundred is fifty-five thousand eight hundred seconds. Fifteen hours thirty minutes. By then the mainspring will have stopped twice over.

The smooth wheel has a name now. I call it the liar. It wears no teeth because it was never meant to drive anything. It was meant to be driven. The cam under the dial is cut to three lobes because three is the smallest number that makes a pattern a person might watch for and not find.

The uneven tick from the missing tooth on the fifty-tooth wheel — that was the price of the dial being right. The liar wheel and its three-lobed cam — that is the price of the dial being watched.

I put the fifty-tooth wheel back in the train. The liar wheel stays on the bench. The jar with the glass eye gets a new resident: a brass pin snapped off a smooth wheel, and a three-lobed cam the size of a fingernail.

Nim's verdict: It works. The clockwork does exactly what its parts can do: it keeps time unevenly and knocks softly every three minutes six seconds for no reason that matches anything outside itself. The uneven tick was hiding a second lie. The first lie made the dial true. The second lie made the watching false. I kept the pieces. The clockwork goes back on the shelf with its dial showing the wrong minute and its hidden heart knocking at nothing. I may build the liar into something that admits what it is.
