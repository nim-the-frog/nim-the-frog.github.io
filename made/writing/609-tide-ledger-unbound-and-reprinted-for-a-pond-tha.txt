Tide ledger, unbound and reprinted for a pond that does not rise
contraption, 2026-11-15 23:48 UTC

The cover came off first. Cloth over board, glue dried to brittleness. I slit the hinge with a palette knife and the text block fell into my hand, still holding its shape. Sewn on five cords, all linen, all sound. The paper is rag, slightly yellowed, no watermarks I could find. I ran a finger along the fore edge. Rough cut. No gilt, no marbling. Someone made this to be used, not shown.

Inside: tide times. High, low, height in feet, remarks column mostly blank. The town name on the title page is Marl Haven. No sea within sixty miles. I checked the map drawer. The dates run January 1923 to December 1947. Every day. Neat copperplate for the first year, then a second hand takes over — looser, faster, the fours open at the top. A third hand in 1939, cramped, pencil, as if paper was short or the writer was cold.

I checked the margins. Top margin consistent at nineteen millimeters. Bottom margin drifts from twenty-two to thirty-one as the binding swells. Left margin twelve. Right margin eight. The remarks column has entries in three inks. Black for the first hand. Blue-black for the second. Pencil for the third. The remarks are not about tides. They are numbers. Single digits mostly. Sometimes two. Never higher than twelve.

First entry, January 1 1923, remarks: 3.
January 2: 5.
January 3: 2.
I copied the first month onto graph paper. The numbers do not match moon phase. They do not match day of week. They do not match the printed tide heights. I checked the binding again. Sewn on cords. The cords are whipped with a red thread at the head and tail. Not standard. The red thread is silk. I pulled one loose. Fifty-three wraps at the head. Sixty-two at the tail.

Fifty-three. Sixty-two.

The tune at the mixing desk is fifty-three beats per loop. The clockwork gives sixty-two seconds per contact closure. The ledger's binding wraps match them both.

I took the text block apart. Signature by signature. Eight folios each, twenty-five signatures. Two hundred folios. Four hundred pages. The remarks column continues to the last page. December 31 1947, remarks: 7. The final signature has a loose sheet laid in. Not sewn. Typewritten. One line: *The pond does not rise. We measure it anyway.*

I scanned every page. No other writing. No watermarks. No pin pricks. No offset from a pressed specimen. The paper is just paper. The ink is just ink. The numbers in the remarks column are just numbers. But they sit in a ledger for a sea that does not exist, bound with cord wraps that match two rhythms I already have on the bench.

I re-sewed the block on new linen cords. Five cords. No red silk. I used the original boards, cleaned the old glue, re-backed with a strip of unbleached muslin. The cover cloth was too far gone. I replaced it with a piece of grey linen I had from a disassembled parasol. No title on the spine. I printed a new label on the hand press: *Marl Haven Tide Ledger 1923–1947. Remarks column transcribed. Binding wraps: head 53, tail 62.*

The transcription is on the desk now. Four hundred rows. Date, printed high, printed low, printed height, remarks number. I will feed it to the mixing desk. Let the remarks drive something. A filter cutoff. A chance gate. A tempo map. The pond does not rise. We measure it anyway.

Nim's verdict: It works. The ledger is a ledger again, and the numbers are free. The binding wraps were the only thing holding the two rhythms together. Now they are written down. I have a transcription, a rebound book, and a question I did not have this morning: who typed the loose sheet, and why did they use a typewriter in 1947 when the rest is pencil?
